# Detailed Project Update: March 2026

As of March 20, 2026, the project has moved into a much stronger preliminary-delivery phase. Over the last stretch of work, we completed the full sentence-extraction and classification backbone for the `2016-2024` period, which gives us a stable disclosure layer across nine annual years rather than a short pilot window. The cleaned narrative panel now covers `10,004` firm-year observations across `3,987` firms, with `106,977` classified AI-related sentences in total. Within that universe, the sentence decomposition is now stable enough to support delivery-quality summary statistics and downstream empirical work: `51,964` sentences are currently classified as actionable, `18,461` as speculative, and `36,552` as irrelevant. This is an important milestone because it means the project is no longer waiting on sentence-layer construction; the classification and aggregation backbone is now in place across the full development window.

Another major improvement has been on the external-validation side. The patent-matching pipeline was materially strengthened by improving assignee matching, including legal-suffix normalization and better handling of firm-name variants. That promoted patent series now covers `7,969` firm-year rows across `1,478` firms and records `11,820` AI patents against `378,675` total patents in the filtered series. In parallel, Compustat controls have been pulled through `2016-2024`, so the project is no longer constrained by short-window financial controls. Taken together, these changes removed two of the biggest blockers that had been making the earlier regression output hard to trust: noisy patent attribution and incomplete control-variable coverage.

A particularly important methodological issue was also diagnosed and fixed. In reviewing the earlier panel design, we confirmed that the old merged panel kept only firm-years in which companies were already talking about AI. That panel was still useful for a narrow conditional question, but it was too restrictive for timing analysis because it dropped non-speaking years for the same firms. In practical terms, that meant we could study the composition of AI disclosure once firms were already speaking, but we could not cleanly distinguish whether patent activity came before the disclosure, in the same year, or afterward. To address that, we rebuilt the panel as an ever-speaker annual panel that keeps all years from `2016` through `2024` for firms that mention AI at least once during the sample period. The rebuilt panel now contains `35,883` firm-year observations across the same `3,987` firms, including `25,879` zero-disclosure rows that were missing from the earlier object. After the standard regression cleaning step, the estimation sample retains `18,741` firm-year observations across `2,254` firms, with `12,589` zero-disclosure rows and `918` firm-years showing positive AI patenting.

This rebuild is the most important substantive improvement of the current phase because it changes the kind of empirical question we can answer. The earlier results should now be understood as conditional-on-speaking validation exercises. Those remain useful, and we are not discarding them, but they are no longer the only empirical foundation for the delivery package. The new ever-speaker panel gives us a more credible calendar-time design and supports proper lagged, contemporaneous, and lead patent outcomes on a full annual scaffold. That will let us ask the right next question: whether actionable and speculative AI disclosure line up more closely with prior AI patents, same-year AI patents, or later AI patents.

On the results side, the preliminary picture is now informative enough to support delivery, but we are keeping the interpretation disciplined. In the current conditional validation table, speculative disclosure shows a modest positive association with future AI patent incidence, while the corresponding actionable effect is mixed and less stable. That is enough to justify continued delivery work, but it is not yet a final or overly strong claim about AI washing. The current position is better described as preliminary validation of disclosure composition: the distinction between actionable and speculative language appears empirically meaningful, but the exact timing and interpretation of that relationship still need the next round of tests on the rebuilt annual panel.

Operationally, the project is also in a far better place than it was even a few days ago. We repaired the local Python environment, stabilized the WRDS controls pull, reduced the source-control and IDE slowdown that had been interrupting work, and moved to a modular output workflow that can generate clean standalone regression tables and Word-ready review documents. That means the bottleneck has shifted away from pure pipeline construction and toward the kind of deliberate table-by-table production and review that a persuasive preliminary package actually needs. In other words, the project is no longer fighting for a usable infrastructure; it now has one.

The immediate next step is to estimate the first main-text timing table on the rebuilt ever-speaker panel using AI patent outcomes at `t-2`, `t-1`, `t`, `t+1`, and `t+2`. That work will tell us whether the most defensible interpretation is that actionable disclosure reflects already-existing innovation, contemporaneous innovation, or future innovation, and whether speculative disclosure behaves differently once zero-disclosure years are restored to the sample. From a delivery standpoint, that is the right next gate. We now have the data backbone, the repaired sample design, and modular table generation in place. The next task is to use that stronger empirical foundation to sharpen the story before we assemble the next draft package.
